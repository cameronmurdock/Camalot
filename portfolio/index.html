Rivr

A federated coordination platform, and the site that explains it

Rivr is a social coordination network: events, groups, offerings, jobs and a shared map, with governance and a treasury for the groups that need them. I lead its technology, product and infrastructure.

It is federated. A hub connects sovereign person and group instances that keep their own data, and peers trade signed events instead of trusting one central database. An assistant inside the app works through the same permissions as the person it acts for.

The landing site at rivr.social is static HTML that I ported from Squarespace and serve from the same infrastructure. This site is published from my own Rivr instance, which is why the homepage can list live objects from it.

Social post planner

Post design, scheduling and delivery, automated

A planner for whoever runs a group’s social accounts. A post starts as a photo and a caption; the planner shows where it will sit on the profile and how the platform will frame it, because a grid that lies about framing is worse than no grid.

Once a time is set, a publishing run delivers the post to each destination and records what happened per channel. A post can land on one platform and fail on another, and the person sees which, along with whether the fix is a new token or a different post.

Each account is reached with a token its owner pastes in. Tokens are encrypted at rest, checked against the platform the moment they are saved, and never written to a log or an error message.

The crop the platform will actually use

Instagram crops twice. A feed picture is first cut to fit between 4:5 and 1.91:1, then the profile grid shows a centred square of that.

The planner computes both crops the way Instagram does and renders the photo through them, so a subject that will be cut in half looks cut in half before it is posted.

A post goes out once

  1. Claim before delivering. Two runs racing means one wins the write, so one delivers.
  2. A claim that never came back is never retried. It waits for a person to check.
  3. Every delivery carries a key that stays the same for the life of the post, so the platform can refuse a repeat too.

The order is the schedule

Live and planned posts share one grid, newest first, exactly as a profile lays them out.

Dragging a square moves the post between time slots and leaves the slots alone. “One a day at nine” stays one a day at nine however often the plan is rearranged.

Credentials and account ownership

A written protocol for who holds the keys

A small site still touches a registrar, DNS, a host, a code repository and usually an AI subscription. If those sit in the developer’s accounts, the client does not own their own website. I write down who owns each one and how it reaches the client before anything is deployed.

The protocol covers where secrets live, how narrowly each credential is scoped, how DNS moves without breaking mail, what to do when something leaks, and the order of the final handoff. The order matters: new credentials are proven to work before the old ones are revoked.

Principle
The client owns every account. I hold access only while I am working.

Secrets never enter the repository

secrets.local/        # git-ignored
  server.env
  oracle-ssh.pem
  cloudflare-api-token
  oci/
    config
    oci_api_key.pem

chmod 700 secrets.local
chmod 600 secrets.local/*

Anything pasted into a chat, an issue or a commit is treated as leaked and rotated.

Each key opens one door

  • A DNS token limited to editing records on the client’s two zones, never the account-wide key.
  • A dedicated cloud user with a narrow policy, not an administrator’s key.
  • SSH limited to the administrator’s address; only web ports face the internet.
  • The editing assistant has no shell, no secrets and no deploy rights. Deploys are a separate, signed step.

Handing the keys over

  1. Keep the repository free of secrets.
  2. Send the secrets folder through a password manager or an encrypted archive, with the password on a different channel.
  3. Move the cloud tenancy, registrar, DNS zones, code repositories and AI subscription into accounts the client controls.
  4. Issue fresh credentials under the client’s ownership.
  5. Swap them into the running service and confirm it is healthy.
  6. Only then revoke my old tokens.
  7. Delete every unencrypted transfer copy.

Moving DNS without an outage

  • Copy every existing record first: mail, verification, DKIM and DMARC included.
  • Keep web records unproxied until the new origin serves TLS.
  • Add staging hosts as new records instead of changing live ones.
  • Leave a domain where it is until its email forwarding is documented and reproduced.

If something leaks

  1. Stop the worker and the container.
  2. Rotate the affected credential.
  3. Read the cloud cost and audit logs, the repository history and the DNS audit log.
  4. Preserve the logs, then restore service.

A one-dollar budget with alerts at one cent catches a runaway bill before it is a bill.

The Riverside

Systems integration, admin APIs and the front ends on top

The Riverside is an events venue in Boulder. Over four years there I integrated point-of-sale, payroll, shift management, product production and venue booking into one connected operational system, replacing disconnected manual tracking.

The backbone is a set of Cloudflare Workers on one domain. Webhooks from the booking and scheduling tools land in the shared record within seconds. A public events site builds itself from that record, sells tickets through Stripe, and writes each sale and guestbook entry back against the event.

I also built much of the current public-facing websites for The Riverside and The Coffee Stand, connecting the venue, café and events experience online.

On top of the data sit the pieces people touch: the events pages, a guestbook, a shift checklist for staff, and Rivertree, an app that turned the venue’s points-for-tasks model into software.
